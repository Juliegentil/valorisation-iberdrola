Football field

Each bar is the value range produced by one method. The black line is the share price at 31 December 2025, the green line the average of the three methods.

Discounted cash flow

Project the cash the business will generate, then discount it back to today. Result: €16.9 to €20.0. The share price sits in the middle.

Sum of the parts

Value the networks and the generation fleet separately, at prices buyers have actually paid for comparable assets. Result: €16.3 to €18.8. The share price sits at the top of the range.

Trading comparables

Apply to Iberdrola the multiples at which Enel, Engie and RWE trade. Result: €8.5 to €17.1. The share price sits well above.

The conclusion. Two methods out of three say the price is justified. The third says Iberdrola is valued far above its peers. Both are true at once: the market accepts the premium because a growing share of Iberdrola's earnings is set by public regulators rather than exposed to power prices.

Why the German Bund rather than the Spanish bond. The risk-free rate is the yield of the strongest borrower in the currency area, which is Germany. Country risk is not ignored: it sits in the equity risk premium, weighted by where the assets actually are. Why this method is not sufficient on its own

Iberdrola reinvests 62.5% of its EBITDA into new infrastructure. That leaves only €1,664m of genuinely free cash in 2025, out of €15,956m of EBITDA. The four projected years therefore carry little weight.

The mechanical consequence: 92% of the result comes from what is assumed after 2029. That part depends on only two unverifiable numbers, the cost of capital and the long-term growth rate. Because the two are close to each other, a half-point move in either shifts the result by 13%.

A longer version of the model, over ten years, brought that share down to 76%. But the six additional years were produced by simple interpolation between the published guidance and the terminal assumption, with no real information behind them. The 92% is the honest measure of how much the terminal assumption carries.

On the normalised year: capex is brought down to the level of depreciation, meaning the group maintains its asset base without expanding it. That is the condition for applying the perpetuity formula. Applying it to a year in which the group still invests 1.5 times depreciation would mean deducting growth capex forever while never counting its returns.

Sum of the parts means valuing each business separately, at prices comparable assets have actually changed hands for, then adding them up and deducting the debt.

Two reference prices, both observed

Networks: 1.45x

In August 2024 Iberdrola acquired Electricity North West, a distribution network in north-west England. Enterprise value for 100% of the company: £4.2bn. Regulatory asset value: £2.9bn. Iberdrola therefore paid 1.45 times that value, a 45% premium.

Generation: 18.0x

In December 2024 Brookfield acquired Neoen, a French renewable energy producer, for around €6.1bn. That represented 18 times its annual EBITDA.

Why networks are valued on their assets rather than their earnings

An electricity network is a natural monopoly: nobody builds two competing networks down the same street. A public regulator therefore sets how much the operator is allowed to earn, on a simple rule: allowed revenue equals the asset value recognised by the regulator, multiplied by a rate of return the regulator sets, plus depreciation and operating costs.

A network's earnings are therefore not an independent variable: they follow mechanically from the value of its assets. Multiplying one or the other amounts to the same thing, and the whole sector works from the asset base. Iberdrola publishes that figure country by country, and it is the basis on which it bought Electricity North West.

Paying 1.45 times means buying €100 of regulator-recognised assets for €145. The 45% premium is justified if the buyer expects to beat the allowed return through efficiency, if the asset base is set to grow, or if there are synergies with a network already owned. Iberdrola already owned the two neighbouring networks in Scotland.

Below 1.00 times, the market would be doubting the operator's ability to earn the return the regulator grants it.

Which multiples are observed and which are assumptions

Two of the four multiples are prices actually paid in transactions: 1.45 times regulated assets for Electricity North West, and 18 times EBITDA for Neoen. They are not argued for, they are observed.

The other two, 6 times on customer supply and 6 times on corporate costs, are assumptions. Customer supply is an asset-light, highly competitive, low-margin activity: it does not command a high multiple. Corporate costs are valued at the same multiple for consistency.

Those two lines account for €15,276m out of €188,336m, or 8% of the total. Even doubling their multiple moves the value per share by less than one euro. The result therefore rests mainly on observed prices, not on judgement calls.

The strongest cross-check in this work

Reverse the logic. Start from the market price, strip out the generation fleet at the Neoen multiple and the supply business, and see what is left for the networks.

The market implicitly values Iberdrola's networks at 1.40 times their regulated asset base.

Iberdrola paid 1.45 times for Electricity North West. Two entirely independent routes, 3% apart.

In other words: Iberdrola's share price at the end of 2025 corresponds to what its assets would cost if bought one by one, at prices paid in real transactions. It is the most robust cross-check in the whole exercise.

The three multiples. EV/EBITDA divides enterprise value, market capitalisation plus net debt, by earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: what the whole business costs per euro it generates. P/E divides the share price by earnings per share: what a euro of shareholder profit costs. EV/Sales divides enterprise value by revenue.
Why EV/Sales is not used here. In this sector revenue includes energy bought for resale, largely through trading. It measures throughput, not the size of the business. Iberdrola reports €45.5bn of revenue for €16.6bn of EBITDA, a 36% margin, while Engie reports €71.9bn for €14.7bn, a 20% margin. Applying the peer median would give €3.17 per share, a meaningless figure that says nothing except that Iberdrola trades less energy than its peers. RWE's revenue could not be sourced reliably.

Why EV/EBITDA and P/E diverge so far

One measurable fact explains the gap: Iberdrola converts a far higher share of its EBITDA into net income than its peers. It carries less depreciation and lower financial charges per euro earned. EV/EBITDA stops before those lines and cannot see the advantage; P/E, built on net income, captures it in full.

Two reservations. RWE's net income used here is its adjusted figure, while Enel's and Engie's are reported: its P/E is therefore not strictly comparable. And E.ON was excluded from the panel: its year-end closing price is known, €16.125 at 30 December 2025, but neither its share count nor its year-end net debt could be traced to a primary source. Estimating them would have distorted the median.

The shares were sharply re-rated in 2025

The share price moved from €13.30 at the end of 2024 to €18.46 at the end of 2025, up 38.8% in a year. Earnings did not grow nearly as fast: this is a re-rating by the market, not an equivalent improvement in results.

EV/EBITDA or P/E: which to trust

EV/EBITDA is the standard multiple in M&A, because it measures what the whole business costs, debt included, against what it generates before depreciation, interest and tax. A buyer assumes the debt, so it is the right measure from their point of view.

Its weakness here: EBITDA is consolidated at 100% while part of the subsidiaries belongs to other shareholders. At Iberdrola those minority interests are worth €13.4bn, at Enel €12.4bn. Data providers do not include them in enterprise value, so numerator and denominator do not cover the same perimeter. The bias is identical across the panel, which keeps the relative comparison valid even if the absolute level is approximate.

P/E does not have that problem: market capitalisation covers only Iberdrola's own shares, and the earnings figure is already net of minorities. Its own weakness is high sensitivity to one-off items in any single year. Enel illustrates the point: its 2025 net income of €4,261m is well below the €7,016m of 2024, which mechanically inflates its ratio.

Neither wins outright. That is why the range used runs from one to the other, €8.48 to €17.11, rather than to a single figure.

Iberdrola, S.A.

Founded in 1840, headquartered in Bilbao, listed on the Madrid stock exchange. More than 44 GW of installed renewable capacity at the end of 2025, alongside nuclear and thermal capacity. Present in Spain, the United Kingdom, the United States, Brazil and several other markets.

Three businesses, and a shift

Iberdrola does three things. It transmits and distributes electricity through networks whose tariffs are set by public regulators. It generates electricity, largely renewable. And it sells it to end customers.

The centre of gravity has moved. Networks now generate 54% of operating profit, against 52% in 2022, and absorb half of capital expenditure. The stated target is 55% of EBITDA by 2028. Iberdrola is no longer a green energy producer that happens to own networks: it is a network operator that happens to own generation.

Geographically, Spain accounts for 36% of revenue, Brazil 20%, the United States 19% and the United Kingdom 16%. But looking only at regulated networks, the United Kingdom and the United States together account for two thirds of the asset base.

Stated strategy

The 2025-2028 plan provides for €58bn of investment, around €37bn of it in networks, with 70% of network investment directed to the United Kingdom and the United States. The target is €18,000m of EBITDA in 2028, with close to 75% of earnings coming from regulated activities or long-term contracts.

Recent transactions follow the same logic. The acquisition of Electricity North West in the United Kingdom in August 2024 made the UK the group's largest regulated market. Mexico was sold, first 55% of the business for around $6.2bn in February 2024, then thirteen thermal plants in 2026. And, after the valuation date used here, Iberdrola agreed in July 2026 to acquire 80% of Caruna, Finland's largest electricity distributor, for around €5bn.

Dividend

Iberdrola commits to distributing 65% to 75% of net income, with a floor of €0.64 per share guaranteed through 2028. The dividend for 2025 is €0.685 per share, for a total distribution of €4.5bn, up 12%.

One feature worth knowing: part of the shareholder base takes the dividend in shares rather than cash. That creates new shares each year and therefore dilutes earnings per share. In 2025 there were two scrip issues, a capital reduction of 200 million shares, and a capital increase of 331 million shares raising €5bn.

The European electricity sector

Companies that generate, transmit, distribute and sell electricity. This perimeter determines which companies Iberdrola is compared with, and therefore a large part of the valuation outcome.

Four activities, four different economics

Generation sells power at market prices: cyclical, capital-hungry and risky. Transmission and distribution are monopolies whose tariffs are set by a regulator: predictable revenue, political risk. Customer supply needs little capital but earns thin margins.

The direct consequence: two companies in the same sector, with the same revenue, are not worth the same if one earns 60% of it from regulated activities and the other 30%. That is exactly what the panel shows: Enel and Engie trade at around 6.4 times EBITDA, RWE at 8.5 times, and Iberdrola at 10.4 times.

How a network makes money

Nobody builds two competing electricity networks down the same street. It is therefore a monopoly, and a public regulator sets how much the operator may earn: the value of its assets multiplied by an allowed rate of return, plus depreciation and operating costs.

Three counter-intuitive consequences. Power prices have almost no effect on a network's revenue. To earn more you must grow the asset base, which means investing: capital expenditure is the engine of earnings growth, the opposite of a conventional industrial company. And the main risk is not commercial, it is political.

What drives demand

Electrification of end uses. Electric vehicles, heat pumps, industry. Electricity demand is rising while total energy demand is flat.

Data centres. E.ON has signed an agreement with CyrusOne to supply up to 61 MW near Frankfurt. RWE sold a UK data centre project to a hyperscaler, booking a €225m gain. EDP cites this demand as an earnings driver through 2028.

The point to remember: the constraint is not generation capacity, it is grid connection. Endesa had to turn down a significant share of the connection requests it received in 2025 for lack of network capacity.

Where the cycle stands

The sector is emerging from a violent sequence: the energy price spike of 2022, normalisation through 2023 and 2024, and rising interest rates throughout. The main headwind today is the level of rates: the German 10-year yield has moved from around 2.7% to 3.51% in a year, in a sector levered at two to three times EBITDA.

The response is the same everywhere. Shift towards regulated earnings: Iberdrola targets 55% of EBITDA from networks by 2028, E.ON reports 80% of results regulated or contracted, Endesa 85%. Sell minority stakes in generation assets to fund growth without stretching the balance sheet, as practised by Iberdrola, EDPR, RWE and Endesa. And acquire networks, the dominant M&A theme in the sector.

The listed universe is thinning: Neoen acquired by Brookfield, Encavis and Albioma by KKR, EDF renationalised. Infrastructure funds are taking these assets private because they believe the equity market undervalues them.

An observation beyond Europe

Companies in the same sector listed in the United States trade materially higher than their European counterparts, on the order of 40% to 50% on the main multiples. Three explanations are put forward: regulation seen as more predictable and better remunerated, stronger demand growth driven by data centres, and a deeper institutional investor base for listed infrastructure.

This observation does not feed the valuation used here, which compares Iberdrola with its European peers on figures struck at 31 December 2025. But it sheds light on the premium Iberdrola commands over that panel: with two thirds of its regulated assets in the United Kingdom and the United States, it already sits halfway between the two markets.
